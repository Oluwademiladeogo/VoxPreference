VoxPreference: a design review of the automated Preference Grammar tool

Demilade Bickersteth · Covenant University · 20 October 2026

Prof. C.U.C. Ugorji's Preference Grammar picks a preferred pronunciation from the forms speakers actually use, by ranking them on six tenets [1, 3]. Done by hand it takes a great many hours, so the team set out to automate it: our ICAEISD 2024 paper and slides [3], Prof's framework for the automation [P2], the PRD [P1], and the user flow in Figure 1 [P3]. My final-year project was one block of that plan, the speech-to-text conversion [8].

This report reviews the whole planned tool, component by component: what the plan said, what made sense, what did not work and why, and what we found and built instead. Each finding points to a published source [n] or to a test in the repository that anyone can rerun (En); both lists are at the end. The tool now runs end to end, from recordings to the operability table, is live on the web, and is reliable today for voiceless and voiced <th> and the KIT vowel (E4).

1. Preference Grammar, in Ugorji's terms

Ugorji proposed PG for codifying the phonological corpora of New Englishes [1, 2]. It has three components [3]. The input is every attested form of a sound, in its words, as speakers produce it, with no underlying forms. The parameters are the six tenets: International acceptance, Contrastiveness, Frequency, Disambiguity, Phonetic simplicity and Pedagogic convenience, ranked by default as I-Ac >> ContR >> FreQ >> DisaM >> Pho-S >> Ped-C. The ranking is strict but violable, as in Optimality Theory [5], and a user may promote, demote or delete a parameter [3]. The output is the preferred forms, the candidates that earn the fewest violations, and the working is shown in an operability table. The published evaluations are think, where [θɪŋk] beats [tɪŋk], [sɪŋk] and [zɪŋk] [1: 58; 3], and the GOAT vowel, where [o] wins [4]. One outcome of PG is a Nigerian national model of the vowels [3].

So the automated tool has one job: take recordings to attested forms, attested forms to marks on six ranked tenets, and marks to the operability table and the preferred form. Any automation has to reproduce Ugorji's two worked evaluations.

2. What we found, in short

  • Speech to text, then text to IPA, gives dictionary pronunciations. That answers "what was said", while PG asks "how was it said" [3, 8]. Because the output always looked plausible, it also hid the training problems (section 4, component 4).
  • Clustering was asked to discover pronunciations from features that also carry the speaker and the microphone [23].
  • The tenets had definitions but no evidence rules. The published marks are the analyst's judgement [4], so nothing could be ranked automatically until each tenet had a rule (E3).
  • Success was measured by word error rate. For PG what matters is how well the variant shares are estimated, checked against human labels with kappa [15] (E4).
  • The infrastructure was planned before the method had been shown to work [P1].

Hear the sound directly, give every tenet its evidence, and measure against human ears.

3. The planned tool

The aim was right. Most of what went wrong sat between the boxes: what each one had to hand to the next.

4. Component by component

Input

1 User input
The planA web system where a user uploads speech and sets a tenet preference [P2]; the PRD adds user stories for researchers, educators, developers, policy makers and learners [P1].
Made sensePutting the user's ranking at the centre. PG lets a user promote, demote or delete parameters [3], so the tool has to let them.
What did not work, and whyFive audiences and their features were planned before a single analysis had run end to end [P1]. That spread the effort thin at the start.
What we found, and what the tool does nowOne page for one job: words, recordings and a ranking in; the operability table and the preferred form out. The other audiences can build on it once it is trusted.
2 Target word and priority ranking
The planThe user gives a target word and a priority ranking; the ranking is applied at matrix processing [P3].
Made sensePG's input is attested forms within their lexical contexts [3], and the word is that context. The ranking belongs to the user.
What did not work, and whyOne word yields few tokens. The plan did not say which sound in the word is under study, and a ranking kept as a plain list cannot pool two tenets or delete one, which PG allows [3].
What we found, and what the tool does nowSeveral words that share a sound; the sound is chosen from a list (or inferred) and each one is marked validated or not. The ranking editor reorders, deletes and pools tenets; pooled and re-ranked evaluations are tested (E3).
3 Audio files
The planAccept large continuous speech files [3]. Prof's framework reviews continuous speech separation for overlapping talk [P2].
Made senseReal speech from many speakers, including long recordings, is what PG needs.
What did not work, and whyEach file was treated as one voice: the original service returned one transcription per file (E2), and speaker separation was reviewed [P2] but not yet designed. A podcast panel would have counted as one speaker.
What we found, and what the tool does nowAny common audio format, voice notes recorded in the browser, and podcasts. Speaker separation with ECAPA-TDNN [12, 13] credits each word to the person who said it; on 8 held-out conversations it found the right number of speakers every time (E6).

Speech to text conversion: my final-year project

4 ASR processing
The planPRD: a CNN-RNN trained on Colab with MFCC features [P1]. My thesis: wav2vec 2.0 [9] fine-tuned on Nigerian English, SLR70 (5.77 hours, 3,359 utterances, 31 speakers) [8, 18].
Made senseFine-tuning a pretrained wav2vec 2.0 model on public Nigerian recordings was a sound, current choice, and better than training a CNN-RNN from scratch on about 6 hours of audio. A random 90/10 split and word error rate are the usual practice in speech-recognition work, and the thesis measured exactly what it set out to measure [8].
What did not work, and whyIn hindsight, three things stopped the fine-tune from learning, none of them visible in the output. (a) The script lowercased the transcripts (E2), but the model's vocabulary has only capital letters, so all 174,915 label characters in the training half became <unk> and none was a real letter (E1). (b) The installed transformers 4.51.3 leaves one weight, masked_spec_embed, uninitialised when the model loads (it prints a warning to that effect) (E1). The published training logs show what followed: at all 37 logged steps the loss reads 0.0, the gradient norm is NaN and the learning rate is 0, over 3,780 steps (E1). Compared tensor by tensor, all 212 weights the published model shares with the stock model are identical, and its one extra weight, masked_spec_embed, holds 41 NaN values out of 768 (E1). (c) The split put all 31 speakers on both sides, and 67 of the 336 validation sentences also occur in training (E1). Re-run on that same 336-utterance validation split, the unchanged stock model scores WER 36.7% with 112 deletions (E1); the thesis reports 41.10% with 112 deletions [8]. With every weight identical, the reported figure describes the stock model; the difference in WER most likely comes from how the transcripts were normalised before scoring. Underneath all three, speech recognition answers which words were said, while PG asks how they were said.
What we found, and what the tool does nowSpeech recognition now only finds the words: Whisper [11] searches unscripted audio, including look-alike words (tree for three), and a letter aligner places the words when a transcript is given. The fine-tuning script is fixed (capital-letter labels, the weight re-initialised, a guard against invalid losses, a speaker-disjoint split) and a 30-step check ran cleanly (loss 1.40 to 0.72). It has not been rerun in full because the tool no longer depends on it; a full run would take 2 to 3 hours per pass on this laptop, or about an hour on a free Colab GPU.
5 Phoneme tokenisation
The planSplit speech into phoneme tokens before clustering [P2, P3].
Made sensePG works on segments inside words, so the tool must find the segment.
What did not work, and whyIn the thesis the tokens came from text: the transcription was split into words (thesis Figure 3.1) [8]. Nothing in the audio was tokenised.
What we found, and what the tool does nowThe pronouncing dictionary [21, 22] says where the sound sits in each word, and the phone model's alignment finds its frames in the audio.
6 IPA mapping
The planMap words or phonemes to IPA: eSpeak in the thesis [8], a dictionary such as CMU in the team's stage plan [P4].
Made senseIPA is the shared notation, and PG tables are written in it. eSpeak is the standard way to turn text into IPA, and the thesis itself presents its IPA output as illustrative, not verified (thesis §4.4) [8].
What did not work, and whyA dictionary gives the citation form. The thesis's Table 4.2 gives there as [ðɛr] [8], and the same entry is returned whether the speaker said [ð] or [d]. PG's input is the attested forms [3], so this step removes the variation PG ranks.
What we found, and what the tool does nowCandidate pronunciations, written in IPA, are scored against the audio by a multilingual phone model [10], the goodness-of-pronunciation approach [14], with a confidence for each token. Each sound carries a validation status from phonetician-labelled speech (section 6) (E4).
7 CSV or VTT file
The planWrite text and IPA to a CSV, a 5×6 matrix in the framework [P2], or a VTT subtitle file [P3].
Made sensePlain files an analyst can open.
What did not work, and whyA VTT file needs word times. The thesis estimated them from the clip's duration (thesis Figure 3.1) [8]; the deployed service returned no word times at all (E2). The 5×6 size came from the matrix, not from the data.
What we found, and what the tool does nowDownloads in CSV, JSON, HTML and subtitle formats, with measured start and end times for every token (E8).

Audio clustering

8 Primary clustering
The planK-means (and spectral clustering) on MFCC features to group similar sounds [P3, 3].
Made senseUnsupervised grouping could find variants without labelled data, a sensible instinct.
What did not work, and whyMFCC features carry the speaker's voice and the recording channel as well as the sound; speaker-identification systems are built on them [23]. Clusters of whole words can therefore follow the speaker more than the pronunciation, and a cluster does not say which variant it holds.
What we found, and what the tool does nowThe phone model's variant label does the grouping: every token is [θ], [t], [s] and so on, with a confidence (E4).
9 Secondary clustering
The planSub-clusters by cosine similarity, at most a 5×6 matrix [3, P4].
Made senseLooking for finer detail inside a variant.
What did not work, and whyThe same limits as above, and the matrix size decided the number of clusters.
What we found, and what the tool does nowAcoustic sub-clusters inside each variant, from the phone model's features. In our checks they barely separate the variants, so the app shows them as exploratory (E7).
10 Matrix showing contrastiveness
The planA confusion matrix of the sub-clusters, used for the operability analysis [3].
Made senseContrast matters to PG: Contrastiveness is one of the six tenets [3].
What did not work, and whyA confusion matrix measures how much clusters overlap acoustically. PG defines Contrastiveness as phonological distinctiveness and Disambiguity as avoiding ambiguity [3]: whether a variant merges a contrast and makes words sound alike.
What we found, and what the tool does nowThe contrast matrix is kept as exploratory (E7). Contrastiveness is marked from the Lingua Franca Core [6] and Disambiguity from pronouncing dictionaries [21, 22]: [t] in think merges 78 common word pairs, such as bath and bat (E3).

Standard pronunciation generation

11 Matrix processing
The planApply the user's ranking to the matrix [P3]; assign a rank to each cluster by quality or relevance [P4].
Made senseThe ranking is the heart of PG [3].
What did not work, and whyPG's published evaluations are the analyst's judgement and give no algorithm for awarding a mark; Jowitt asks on what basis International acceptance is assigned [4]. Without such rules nothing could be ranked automatically, and ranking clusters by quality is a different operation from PG's evaluation [3, 5].
What we found, and what the tool does nowA rule for each tenet, chosen so that Ugorji's think and GOAT evaluations [1, 3, 4] come out exactly, under strict domination as in Optimality Theory [5] (E3). A variant competes once it has at least 3 tokens from 2 speakers. Experts can override any mark.
12 Outputs
The planThe operability table and the standard pronunciation, saved as .txt and .csv; the stage plan adds a "PG operability metric" of accuracy, consistency and usability [P4].
Made senseTwo outputs, exactly as PG defines them [3].
What did not work, and whyAccuracy and usability describe the software. In PG, operability is the evaluation of candidates against the ranked tenets [3], so the two are better kept apart.
What we found, and what the tool does nowBoth outputs, plus warnings when a sound is not validated or the data is thin, and every token listed for checking by ear (E8).
13 Operability table
The planUgorji's tableau: candidates in rows, tenets in columns, violation marks in the cells [1, 3].
Made senseIt is how PG shows its working.
What did not work, and whyNot yet produced by the earlier work.
What we found, and what the tool does nowProduced in PG style, with an OT view. Figure 3 is the think table as the tool prints it, mark for mark as in Ugorji's slide [3] (E3).
14 Standard pronunciation
The planThe winning candidate [P3].
Made sensePG's output component: the preferred forms earn the fewest violations [3].
What did not work, and whyThe plan did not say how sure the winner is.
What we found, and what the tool does nowThe preferred form, its tier (formal standard, allophone or outer norm), and how often it still wins when speakers are resampled and uncertain tokens redrawn (E8).

Evaluation criteria

15 Evaluation criteria: the six tenets
The planInternational acceptance, Contrastiveness, Frequency, Disambiguity, Phonetic simplicity and Pedagogic convenience, ranked I-Ac >> ContR >> FreQ >> DisaM >> Pho-S >> Ped-C [3].
Made senseThese are Ugorji's own definitions and default ranking [1, 3].
What did not work, and whyThe definitions are qualitative, and the sources leave open what evidence decides each mark [4]. Frequency in particular can be read two ways, and the reading changes the winner for think (E3).
What we found, and what the tool does nowA source of evidence for each tenet (section 7): a knowledge base, the Lingua Franca Core [6], the recordings, pronouncing dictionaries [21, 22] and PHOIBLE [16]. Both readings of Frequency are available as a setting.

Around everything

16 API, hosting and infrastructure
The planA Node.js/Express API; AWS S3, RDS (PostgreSQL), MongoDB and a Redis queue; serving on EKS or SageMaker; ONNX; Prometheus, Grafana, CloudWatch and ELK [P1].
Made senseAn API, and containers.
What did not work, and whyThe full estate was planned before the method had been shown to work, and it is more than the analysis needs at this stage.
What we found, and what the tool does nowOne Python service serves the page and the API from one Docker image, live on Hugging Face's free hardware [19] (section 6) (E9). One GitHub repository, deploy on push and a daily keep-awake visit are being set up.

6. Results

How well it hears each sound

The only free Nigerian English recordings with pronunciations transcribed by trained phoneticians are 22 speakers in the Speech Accent Archive [17], all reading the same paragraph: 753 test tokens. The fairest single number is Cohen's kappa [15], agreement beyond chance: a model that always answers the commonest variant scores 0, however good its accuracy looks (E4).

An independent review was asked to break this work, not defend it (E7). It found two calibrations that only looked useful (GOAT and FACE gave every token the same answer), a weak test ("same preferred form as the experts"), and a phone model that leans towards dictionary forms: it hears Nigerian [d] as [ð] 36 times and the reverse once. All are fixed or stated plainly; a calibration is now kept only when kappa rises on speakers it was not fitted on, which leaves voiced <th> alone.

Speaker separation

PG counts speakers, not files. The tool cuts speech into 1.5-second windows, takes a speaker fingerprint of each with ECAPA-TDNN [12, 13], removes what all the fingerprints share (room, microphone, language), averages each window with its neighbours, groups them into speakers, and credits every target word to the person speaking at that moment. It was tested on made-up 4-minute panels of 2 to 5 real SLR70 Nigerian speakers [18], including three and four women together (E6). An earlier model, WavLM, credited under 75% of words correctly for four women even when told the number of speakers; ECAPA was then tuned on 4 conversations and tested on 8 it had never seen.

The tool is live at https://thebickersteth-voxpreference.hf.space on Hugging Face's free CPU hardware (2 cores, 16 GB) [19]. 7. The tenets, and Ugorji's table as the tool prints it

No PG source gives an algorithm; the published marks are the analyst's judgement [4]. The rules below are our proposal, chosen so that both of Ugorji's evaluations [1, 3, 4] come out exactly right (E3). They are defaults the PG experts can change, not PG's own verdicts.

TenetA variant gets a mark whenEvidence
International acceptanceit is not an accepted international norm for the sound (for /θ/: θ, t, t̪, f are; s, z are not)knowledge base: RP and GA, established L1 varieties, the Lingua Franca Core
Contrastivenessit merges a contrast that matters for international intelligibilityLingua Franca Core
Frequencyits share is under 20%, or, in "most frequent" mode, it is not the commonest variantthe recordings
Disambiguityit makes common words sound alike (three and tree)pronouncing dictionaries and word frequency
Phonetic simplicityit is missing from most of Hausa, Igbo and Yoruba, or rare in the world's languagesPHOIBLE
Pedagogic convenienceit is missing from those languages and rarely used by the speakers analysedPHOIBLE and the recordings

Sources: Lingua Franca Core [6]; pronouncing dictionaries [21, 22]; PHOIBLE [16].

What "rank by frequency" means

Many users read "Frequency first" as "the commonest pronunciation wins": if think came out with [θ] 49 times and that was the most, [θ] is preferred. Ugorji's think table only comes out right under another reading, where Frequency removes rare variants (under 20%), because in the ICE-Nigeria shares [t] is slightly more common than [θ] [7]. Both readings are in the tool, and the test suite checks that each gives its expected winner (E3). The default follows Ugorji.

8. Questions for Prof. Ugorji

  • Which norms count as international, and why [t] escapes that mark in think while [s] does not [1, 3]. Jowitt raises the same question [4].
  • What Frequency means: a threshold or "the commonest wins"; per token or per speaker; over whose speech. This alone can flip think to [t] (E3).
  • What separates Contrastiveness from Disambiguity, and what counts as evidence for Pedagogic convenience. The full list of 10 is in backend/pg/RESEARCH.md, section 6.

9. Next steps

  1. Label 150 to 300 new Nigerian tokens, ideally twice, with the listening tool (python -m pg.review). This turns the vowels from not validated to validated.
  2. Take the questions above to Prof. Ugorji before presenting automated tables as PG results.
  3. Add the podcast results and check the speaker separation against the known four-person panel.
  4. Try a stronger model for vowels: the Montreal Forced Aligner's English model, trained partly on Nigerian English, or formant measurements.
  5. Finish the single repository with deploy on push and the daily keep-awake visit.

The method works where it has been checked. The next work is checking more of it.
